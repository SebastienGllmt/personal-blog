Offer Files

How offer files turn a private swap into a plain text file you can paste anywhere - one shared pool of liquidity that no single app owns, carrying everything from a DEX quote that lives for seconds to a listing that waits years for its buyer.

The throughput wall

Privacy comes with a computational cost - you have to generate proofs - and that computational cost comes with a throughput cost. Every zero-knowledge chain - Midnight included - trades raw transactions-per-second for the ability to compute privately. That squeezes an exchange from both sides. The classic order-book design puts every order on chain: placing an order is a transaction, cancelling it is another, and the large majority of resting orders are never filled at all - a lot of block space spent on intentions. The usual way out is an automated market maker, which keeps order flow off chain - but an AMM is a general-purpose smart contract, and on a privacy chain a general contract means a far heavier proof than simply placing an order. Either way, the naive exchange is expensive here.

The modern fix is the intent: you broadcast what you want off chain, and the chain is only touched when a solver actually fills it. Offer Files are this pattern - with a twist that makes them unusually clean on Midnight.

zSwap intro

zSwap: swaps live in the L1

On most chains, swapping two assets means deploying a smart contract. Midnight builds atomic swaps directly into the base layer via a primitive called zSwap. The unit you hold is an offer, and an offer is a deliberately imbalanced transaction: it contributes some assets and withdraws others, and on its own it does not add up.

Concretely, every offer carries a signed amount per token - its deltas map. Alice's offer might read { NIGHT: +5, ROCK: −3 }: it contributes five NIGHT and withdraws three ROCK. The ledger only accepts a transaction in which no token nets negative - in the simple trade, everything cancels to exactly zero - so her offer alone is never valid. It is one half of a trade, waiting for the other half.

Two offers merge by unioning their inputs and outputs and summing their deltas element-wise. Crucially, each offer already embeds its own zero-knowledge proof, so combining them re-proves nothing: the taker needs no proving system, and the merge is fully non-interactive. Complementary halves cancel to a balanced whole that the chain accepts in one shot.

And the match doesn't have to be a perfect mirror. The real rule is the gentler one above - no token may come out negative - so a taker can combine offers that don't quite line up and attach a piece of their own that pockets the difference (the figure shows the move). That's not a loophole - it's the mechanic that makes aggregators and arbitrage routing work. (Strictly, you could even leave the surplus unclaimed - the chain accepts a positive leftover and simply destroys it, crediting no one - which is why in practice you always pocket it explicitly.)

It is tamper-proof by construction. Altering any byte of an offer breaks the embedded proof, rendering it unusable - and the pieces are cryptographically bound together, so no one can strip your coins out for a different trade either (how that binding works is a technical aside, later). You can hand an Offer File to a stranger; the worst they can do is discard it.

zSwap vs. a ZswapOffer

A quick naming aside, because the Midnight codebase is precise about it and the distinction matters here. zSwap is the whole system - the shielded, private token machinery, UTXO-like but backed by commitment and nullifier sets you can never directly enumerate. A ZswapOffer is one concrete structure inside the Midnight codebase: a bundle of inputs (coins spent), outputs (coins created), transients, and a deltas map. It's the unit that gets merged, balanced, and ultimately submitted as part of a transaction.

The privacy model is stronger than people expect. At the level of an individual coin, zSwap hides everything: there are no addresses (ownership is proven in zero-knowledge), and even the asset type and amount are sealed away inside cryptographic commitments. The only party who can read what an output actually holds is its receiver, through a note encrypted just for them. So how does anyone learn what's being traded? One thing is public, by necessity: the offer's deltas - its net imbalance per token ({ NIGHT: +5, ROCK: −3 }), declared in the clear. You must see it to know how to balance the transaction, so it can't be hidden.

Note that the deltas are just a temporary concern - they can be stripped once the transaction is balanced. Picture each offer as a box, with its deltas printed on a label stuck to the outside - public only because you need it to work out how to balance the trade. On its own, an offer's deltas are non-zero; that imbalance is the trade, so the label has to be in the clear.

Now stack several offers whose labels add up, inside one transaction, and watch the running sum. Merging unions their coins and sums the per-token deltas - and when the offers complete each other, every token's running sum lands on zero.

And here's the payoff. Once the sum is zero and the bundle carries a validity proof that it balances, those all-zero labels say nothing the proof hasn't already guaranteed - so they peel off, and the boxes load into one transaction. The intuition is worth holding onto: deltas have to be public so you know how to balance the transaction, but once you have a proof that it is balanced, you don't care about the deltas any more - and they're dropped from the bundle. (How the chain can add up values it cannot see - the commitment trick that also makes merging proven offers cheap - is a technical aside there, for the curious.)

From a swap to a file

If an offer is a self-contained, tamper-proof, mergeable object, why keep it on chain? You don't. The Offer Files MIP serializes a proven offer and encodes it with bech32m, producing a plain ASCII string. That string is the Offer File. It carries a checksum so a copy-paste error can't silently corrupt it.

And here's why that string is safe to share: an offer's trade terms live in its public deltas, while the coins and the parties stay private. So an Offer File reveals what's on offer - "gives 5 NIGHT, wants 3 ROCK" - plus one opaque tag per backing coin (we meet it properly a little later), and nothing else. You can post it in the open and let anyone match it, without leaking who you are or what else you hold. Open trade terms, private everything else - that's the property the rest of this system is built on.

It is not small - the proofs dominate. The offer itself is about 10 KB (10,143 bytes); spelled out as the shareable bech32m string it expands to 16,246 characters (too large for a standard QR code). Only the compact ~10 KB of bytes is ever posted on-chain - the longer string is purely its copy-pasteable form. But it is just text: zswapoffer1qpzry9x8gf2tvdw0s3jn54khce6mua7l… You can paste it into a chat, attach it as a file, or host it behind a URL. No complex setup rquired.

An Offer File stays valid until one of two things happens: the maker spends the coins it was built from (anyone can detect this - how, we'll see a little later), or its time-to-live expires. Both are judged against current chain state - which turns out to be exactly half of the indexing problem we hit later.

Where do offers live?

P2P Offer Files

You can absolutely keep an offer file private and hand it straight to someone - and that alone is useful. Think back to NFT mania, when people traded by posting in Discord: that needed a trusted escrow, because either side could renege. An offer file gives you that same casual peer-to-peer trade with no escrow and more privacy - paste it to a friend and the swap either settles atomically or not at all.

Public Offer Files

But peer-to-peer means only the people you send it to can see it. The bigger prize is shared liquidity: making your offer visible to as many takers as possible. For that our reference design uses Celestia as a public bulletin board - a data-availability layer whose only job is to accept a blob of data and guarantee everyone can download it. It never executes the data - there are no smart contracts here - it merely makes it available.

The killer feature is namespaces. Block data is partitioned into per-application lanes via a namespaced Merkle tree, so a client can sync only the Offer-File namespace rather than the whole chain - and, thanks to the tree's range labelling, still verify that it received every offer in that lane, with none omitted. Combined with data availability sampling, a light node gets close to full-node assurance that the offers are real and complete.

One honest caveat: data availability guarantees the blob was published, not that it is stored forever. Light clients retain roughly a 7-day window - but that isn't a real limitation here, because Midnight's L1 enforces a global two-week time-to-live on offers anyway, so a live offer never outlives the window. Durable history of old offers is something the application arranges itself (a pinned namespace, snapshots, or a paid archive).

Two genuinely hard parts

The theory is clean; two practical problems are not, and the reference implementation exists mainly to solve them. (The offer payload and the indexer API it implements are standardized in MIP-0006: Peer-to-Peer Atomic Swaps, which builds on the Offer Files MIP.)

1. You must watch two chains at once

A correct order book needs both halves of the story. Celestia tells you which offers were created. Midnight tells you which are dead: the instant any coin an offer was built from is spent, that offer can never be filled. The match is mechanical: a spend publishes a tag that the offer file also carries - we unpack it shortly. So you index Celestia for new offers and Midnight for the spends that invalidate them. The reference build uses EffectStream, a framework that lets one program consume both chains as a single ordered stream - new-offer events from Celestia, spent-output events from Midnight - reconciling them into one live book. (The full worked example is its zSwap-DA template.)

2. Users don't hold Celestia's token

Posting a blob to Celestia costs a fee paid in TIA. But a Midnight user holds Midnight assets - expecting them to acquire TIA just to advertise an offer is a non-starter. So EffectStream ships a batcher that stands in the middle: the user hands it the offer, and the batcher submits the blob and pays the TIA fee on their behalf. The user never has to know Celestia is involved. The same tag doubles as the batcher's anti-spam identity: cover the fee once per backing coin, and flooding the batcher means minting fresh coins - each costing the attacker an on-chain spend and, more painfully, zSwap proof-generation time.

The payoff: shared liquidity

Put it together and the trades stop living inside any one application. Every DEX and every NFT marketplace built this way reads from the same pool of Offer Files. An app reduces to two things: a filter over the shared offers and a frontend to present them. Liquidity one site attracts is liquidity every other site can match against - the network effect is no longer captured by a single venue.

That's the technical payoff - one shared pool of liquidity that no single venue owns. But shipping a clean architecture is not the same as earning real trading: “we can build it” and “people will use it” are different claims. The second one deserves an honest answer rather than a hand-wave, and - unusually for a new design - there's a real-world experiment to answer it with. So we return to the business case at the very end, once the design is complete.

Basic terminology

A coin holds exactly one token

A note for those coming from other UTXO chains like Cardano: in Midnight, tokens are not bundled together into a single UTXO. We simply call the contents a Coin. There is no map of asset → quantity inside it, the way a single Cardano UTXO carries ada plus a whole basket of native tokens. So a Coin of ROCK holds only ROCK, with no NIGHT riding alongside it; even NIGHT lives in its own coins. A swap that moves several tokens therefore spends a separate coin for each, and every coin spent or created is its own input or output.

A transaction is just inputs and outputs

Underneath, every Midnight transaction is just a set of inputs and outputs. A ZswapOffer is a proven bundle of some of them - some inputs, some outputs. An offer needn't be two-sided: it can carry several inputs and no outputs, or outputs and no inputs (what you might think of as a "lone" input or output is really just a one-sided offer).

What do you call a private UTXO?

Midnight reserves the word UTXO for the public, unshielded side of the ledger, so the shielded side needs its own vocabulary. A private Coin never appears directly; it shows up as two one-way hashes - one when it is created, one when it is spent:

  • A CoinCommitment is what appears in an output - the Coin hashed together with your public key.
  • A Nullifier is what appears in an input - the same Coin hashed together with your secret key.

One coin, many offer files

It starts with an intuition you already have: an offer file is just text, so nothing stops you posting copies of it in many places at once - a chat, Celestia, a web page. They are all the same offer.

So which thing actually gets spent in a trade? Not the file - the coin underneath it. Loosely, every copy is the same pre-signed spend of one UTXO, and the chain is the only source of truth: the moment that coin is spent on Midnight - by whoever uses any copy, for whatever reason - every copy everywhere transitively stops being spendable. Drive the figure and watch the cascade.

Now take the same picture one step further. Instead of copies of one offer, mint two different offers from the same coin - the same kind of zswapoffer… files as above, just with different terms: say, the same NFT asking 80 NIGHT in one and 70 in the other. The next figure shows the pair - and the little tags riding with them.

That tag is the nullifier: an opaque value, deterministically derived from the backing coin, that each input exposes. It reveals nothing about the coin's contents, but the same coin always produces the same tag, and the tag is published on-chain the moment the coin is spent (it's how the chain blocks double-spends - much more on that later). That gives you two things at once: anyone can check an offer is still live - if its tag has appeared on-chain, the file is dead - and multiple offers from one coin are safely mutually exclusive, the engine behind tricks coming up.

That linkability is a real privacy cost, and the only one this section adds: post offers to two different venues from one coin and observers can tell the same user is behind both. Spreading activity across separate coins restores the separation - at the price of managing more coins.

A few zSwap subtleties

Offers merge into one

Contrary to what you might expect, a transaction in Midnight is not a list of ZswapOffers. Instead, offers can be (cheaply) merged together, and a transaction accepts at most a single ZswapOffer - built by merging any and all offers into one. Merging is defined simply as the union of the fields, where any token that nets to zero simply drops out of the map.

There is one rule: the pieces must be disjoint. Two offers merge only if they share no input, no output, and no transient - which is exactly what you want, since the same coin must not be spent twice in one transaction. The merge checks this and refuses otherwise.

Importantly, merging proves nothing new. Each input and output was already proven when its own offer was built. A merge is just set-union plus addition: no circuit runs, no proof is regenerated.

Technical aside - how hidden values can still be added up and checked, and why that makes merging cheap (most readers can skip this)

The commitments that hide each coin's value are a form of semi-homomorphic encryption: you can't read the hidden value, but you can add two commitments together and get a valid commitment to the sum. That is the whole reason the chain can confirm an offer balances - add up the committed inputs, subtract the outputs and the public deltas, and check that the result cancels to zero - without ever seeing a single amount. It is also why merging needs no new proof: the combined offer's commitments are just the sums of the parts', and they still open to zero if each part did.

Pedersen commitments are one common implementation of this (typically over an elliptic curve, as Midnight uses): a value becomes a curve point, blinded by a random factor so it reveals nothing, yet two such points still add. It's worth flagging because that additive structure is exactly what the L2's recursive proof later leans on - it lets the prover fold and aggregate these commitments across every swap, rather than re-deriving balances from scratch.

So look back at the transaction we drew earlier: what was sketched as three lassoed offers is really one merged offer the instant it becomes a transaction.

The commitment tree

We've leaned on a phrase a few times - ownership is proven in zero-knowledge - without ever saying how. Here's the machinery, built from the coin up. A coin is just data: a value, a token type, and a unique nonce, controlled by a keypair. From it the chain derives two one-way fingerprints, and which key you use decides which one you get. Hash the coin with your public key and you get its commitment, posted the moment the coin is created - "this coin exists." Hash it with your secret key and you get its nullifier, posted the moment it's spent - "this coin is gone." Different keys, so no one can tell the two belong to the same coin; and because only you hold the secret key, only you can mint the nullifier that spends it.

The ledger simply keeps two ever-growing sets: every commitment ever made, and every nullifier ever revealed. A coin is unspent when its commitment is present and its nullifier is not - but since nobody can pair a commitment with its nullifier, nobody can list the unspent coins at all. That uncomputable difference is the whole privacy of the scheme: the chain knows exactly how many coins exist and how many were spent, yet can't point to a single live one.

So how do you spend a coin without revealing which one is yours? You prove it belongs. Every commitment is a leaf of one enormous, append-only Merkle tree - the commitment tree - whose root is a single hash standing in for every coin ever made. To spend, your input carries a zero-knowledge proof of membership: a proof that your coin's commitment sits somewhere under that published root, plus the coin's nullifier, and nothing more. The chain learns the root and the nullifier; it never learns which leaf. That is what "ownership proven in zero-knowledge" comes down to - and a double-spend is caught for free, since the nullifier is already in the set.

One last detail, and it's the one chaining hinges on: the tree only grows when a block is processed. A coin you made a moment ago isn't a leaf yet - no block has folded it in, and the very leaf it will land on depends on whatever other transactions share its block - so it has no root an input could prove membership against.

Chaining needs a transient

Chaining - spending a coin in the same transaction that creates it (we'll see what it unlocks next) - looks like another job for merge. It isn't. Merge is a disjoint set-union: it refuses two offers that share a coin, and it has no notion that one offer's output is the very coin another's input means to spend. Try to merge a producer and its consumer and you don't get a fused coin - you get an output and an input that can never validate.

The reason builds straight on the commitment tree. An input proves its coin is a leaf of that tree, against a published root - but a coin you just created isn't a leaf yet, and won't be until a block folds it in. So an ordinary input spending a brand-new output has no real root to point at.

The fix is a transient: an output and an input for the same coin, fused into a single part of the offer, where the input proves membership not against the global tree but against an ephemeral single-leaf tree holding exactly that one output's commitment - a root you can compute on the spot, before any block exists. (This is the third kind of part in a ZswapOffer, alongside inputs and outputs, that we kept naming and never explained.)

And the price is lopsided. An output proves nothing about any tree, so its proof carries over for free - you reuse it verbatim, even if it was someone else's. The input, though, must be proven against that ephemeral root: a fresh input proof, on the same circuit and at the same cost as spending any coin. So a transient costs about one spend - cheaper than creating the coin and then separately spending it (you save an output proof and a whole settlement), but not free the way a plain merge is. And it has to be arranged at proving time: you prove the input against the single-leaf tree from the start, so you cannot take two already-proven, independent offers and chain them after the fact.

Chaining offers

Spend a coin the moment you make it

Merging combines offers that already stand on their own. But many designs need the opposite: one offer's output is the very coin another offer's input spends, and you want both to happen in the same transaction. zSwap supports this directly. You can create a coin and spend it inside a single transaction, so the coin in the middle is never a free-standing, settled coin of its own - it exists only within the transaction, created and consumed atomically, all or nothing.

That one capability is what a surprising number of projects are really asking for:

  • Multi-hop routing. A router swaps A → B → C in one atomic transaction; the middle coin is created and consumed in flight, never settling on its own.
  • Receive-and-forward. Take a coin - even one someone else just sent you - and immediately re-spend it: split it, forward it, pay out of it, with no round-trip to the chain in between.
  • Multi-step private flows. Assemble several dependent steps as one all-or-nothing transaction, instead of a sequence that could stall or be front-run between settlements.

And it is cheap. Settling that middle coin and spending it later would cost its creating proof, a settlement, then a fresh spend and a second settlement; chaining collapses all of that into one transaction that reuses the creating proof and settles once. It isn't quite free - there's a real proof underneath, and it has to be planned up front rather than bolted on - but those mechanics, and the exact cost, are a subtlety we kept separate: see Chaining needs a transient.

The spectrum

How long does an offer live?

Before building anything bigger, ask the question that quietly decides the engineering: how long does an offer need to stay open before someone takes it?

  • Short-term (seconds–minutes) - a DEX quote, stale the moment the price ticks.
  • Medium-term (days–weeks) - an NFT listing, an auction bid, a launchpad pledge.
  • Long-term (months–years) - a house, or any illiquid asset, waiting for its buyer.

The expected time for an offer to settle ends up driving quite different architectural requirements.

Medium-term is the primitive's native habitat. Midnight's L1 puts a two-week time-to-live on every offer, and Celestia retains about a week of history - so an offer that lives for days to weeks fits the machinery we've built so far with no further work: post the file, wait, settle.

Short-term breaks on cost: shrink the lifetime to seconds and you're cancelling and re-quoting constantly - every cancel an on-chain spend, every fresh quote a new proof. That cost takes over the design; it will take a faster layer, purpose-built, to carry it.

Long-term breaks on time itself: stretch the lifetime to years and the offer expires long before it fills - it dies of old age.

The rest of this post walks this spectrum from the middle out: first the native zone, where the design is already complete; then stretch time out to real estate; then shrink it down to the DEX - the deep end, where most of the remaining engineering lives.

Deployment status, June 2026 - where the two-week TTL actually is today (temporary note)

The two-week ceiling is not a protocol constant - it's a governance parameter (global_ttl), and two weeks is the value it's being raised to. The rollout is in flight: today the preview network still runs the genesis value of one hour, so right now a resting offer there is settleable for at most about an hour. The ledger v9 release raises preview to two weeks (it also ties the chain's Merkle-root retention to the same parameter, so the two limits move together), and mainnet will also increase the limit to two weeks in the next protocol upgrade. Everything this post says about the medium and long zones describes the network as it stands after that rollout.

Medium-term · The native habitat

NFT marketplaces: list once, fill once

A listing is one offer file - the figure shows a real one: give this NFT, want 80 NIGHT. Posting it costs nothing, every marketplace reading the shared pool can display it, and a sale settles in a single transaction - atomic, escrow-free, with buyer and seller hidden.

The defining feature of this zone is cadence. The only way to take a listing down is to spend the coin it was built from - a cancel costs one on-chain transaction. At marketplace pace, where a listing might be repriced once a week, that's nothing; hold the thought, though, because at the fast end of the spectrum this same cost will decide everything.

Even the two-week TTL is right-sized - about the cadence you'd refresh a listing anyway. No L2, no new machinery: for a marketplace, the design from the first half of this post is the product. And it isn't hypothetical - as the Chia receipts show, NFT trading is the single biggest thing offer files have ever carried.

The Cardano precedent: a marketplace before smart contracts

There's a second precedent for this zone, from a chain with no offer files, no atomic swaps, and no privacy: Cardano, which for a stretch of 2021–22 ran one of the largest NFT markets in crypto - in 2022 it broke into the top three chains by 24-hour NFT volume. The part that matters here: Cardano launched NFT trading before the chain had smart contracts. Its tokens are native - an NFT is just an asset sitting in your wallet as a UTXO, like Midnight's - so marketplaces improvised: you sent your NFT to the marketplace's escrow wallet (a custodian), and it handled the sale. That market was real - tens of thousands of sales a month by late 2021 - and it ran for the better part of a year before the first smart-contract marketplace existed. When contracts arrived, they took over within months: a custodian is real risk, and Cardano had nothing between “trust the custodian” and “write a full contract marketplace.”

Don't skip past why people used the custodial bazaar. Contract-less trading was cheap - this was the era when a single Ethereum NFT trade could run to hundreds of dollars in gas - and it felt safe: with no contract in the loop there was nothing to be malicious. On Ethereum at the time, a recurring kind of scam was malicious NFTs that drained wallets when you interacted with them; on Cardano a token was a plain asset in a plain wallet, with no code attached. The one flaw in the model was the custodian itself. That flaw is exactly what an offer file deletes: the same simple, contract-less trade, except the asset never leaves your wallet and the file is tamper-proof instead of trusted. Cardano had to migrate its entire market onto smart contracts to fix custody; with offer files, there is nothing to migrate to.

The same dataset shows what listing behaviour looks like in this zone - the cadence question the whole medium-term argument rests on. Across 1.86M listings: half sold, a third were cancelled (nearly half of those cancels were immediate re-lists - re-prices), and the rest just sat. That is ≈0.6 cancels per sale, against the eight to nine wasted offers per settle Chia's market makers churned. And sales are fast: 55% of everything that sold, sold within 24 hours. A marketplace cancel is a once-a-week decision, so paying one on-chain transaction per cancel barely registers.

And it answers the question the spectrum left hanging: is a two-week TTL actually long enough for this zone? Cardano listings never expired, so we can replay history with an expiry imposed: a 14-day window would have missed just 13.2% of eventual sales - and the market was speeding up, the 14-day miss rate falling from 18% in 2021 to 9% by mid-2022. Roughly seven sales in eight fit inside one offer file's lifetime; the patient tail is handled by a pattern coming up in the long-term zone, the refresh series.

So the two precedents stack. Chia proves offer files clear real trades, for years. Cardano proves the demand: a major NFT market grew up happily with no smart contracts at all - it just had to pay a custodian for the privilege, and it left the moment something trustless existed. Offer files are that same simple, contract-less trade with the custodian deleted - which is why this zone needs no migration story at all.

Auctions: the bids are offer files

Auctions flip the flow. So far, the maker posts and takers compete to fill; in an auction, the bids are the offer files. Each bidder sends the seller an offer, and at close the seller settles exactly one: the best. Losing bids never touch the chain; they expire, or their owners spend the coins behind them (which is withdrawal). If you've traded NFTs you've seen this flow: each bid is what marketplaces call an item offer - a bid on something nobody listed - and pointed at a whole collection it's a collection offer. An auction is just gathering item offers and taking the best.

Look how much trust evaporated. No escrow: a bidder's funds sit in their own wallet the whole time, merely committed. The seller can't take more than the winning bid grants, the winner can't renege after settlement, and no third party can intercept the NFT - the same tamper-proofness from the start of the post, doing auction-shaped work.

Two honest footnotes. deltas are public, so every bid is readable - that makes this an open-outcry English auction; a sealed-bid auction needs machinery offer files don't natively have. And a bid is backed by a specific coin: keeping it open means leaving that coin untouched, and spending it kills the bid silently.

Launchpads: pledges for a token that doesn't exist yet

The launchpad pushes the auction's inversion to its limit. On the marketplace you listed a thing you have; in the auction, people bid on a thing nobody listed; a launchpad collects bids for a token that doesn't exist yet. Each buyer creates an offer - gives 50 NIGHT, wants 1,000 LAUNCH - against a token that has never been minted. The offers pile up off-chain; the moment the pile crosses the sale's target, the project mints the token and accepts them all.

It's worth dwelling on why that's a big deal, because it fixes something genuinely annoying about launchpads across crypto. The standard model makes you lock your $300 in a contract and wait two weeks - dead money, and no reason to revisit the site. Here a bid is a pledge, and the same-coin trick makes pledges composable: pledge the same $300 to five sales at once; the first to close takes the money and the rest die automatically - so you keep coming back for new projects even with your capital committed. Notice what the old model did to the market, too: locked capital pushes launches to be short - every locked day is a day you can't chase the next sale - which is part of what drove the market toward pump.fun-style memecoin launches (dump and re-invest within hours) over serious projects that need longer raises. Pledges remove that pressure: capital is never locked, so a sale can afford to take its time.

And the reason a pledge means anything at all: on a typical chain, a pledge is a promise that everyone must come back and sign when the sale closes - and most never do. On Midnight, a pledge is a zSwap: already proven, already executable, settled by the project without the buyer ever re-approving. (The visible tag cuts both ways here: a sale can see which of its pledges are simultaneously pledged elsewhere, and judge how oversubscribed it really is.)

Now the honest challenge - and it's generic to any sale settled with offer files. A transaction can only carry so many zSwaps - roughly 18 today, fewer if it does anything else - so a sale with thousands of pledges cannot settle atomically; settlement rolls out across many transactions. That opens a griefing window: while the rollout runs, any not-yet-settled pledge can still vanish - its owner spends the backing coin, deliberately or by accident - and the sale closes with less than it counted on.

You can live with that cheaply - oversubscribe the sale, settle the largest pledges first, or simply accept the shortfall risk. Or solve it systematically: once the sale enters the danger zone (say, past 80%), start settling pledges immediately - but for tickets, not tokens. The machinery is the same ~18-zSwap batching as before, just started early: each batch converts a slice of the pledges into tickets while the sale still runs, and a settled ticket can no longer be withdrawn - so the griefing window shrinks with every batch. Then, when the sale reaches 100%, the last step switches on: a small smart contract (written in Compact, Midnight's contract language) that converts tickets one-for-one into the real token.

That last move is worth naming, because it's a pattern that generalizes well beyond launchpads. Offer files get you most of the way - the listing, the bids, the pledges, the early settlement - on a primitive that needs no contract at all, which is exactly what you want while bootstrapping: nothing to audit, nothing to deploy, no contract bugs to ship. Then you reach for a Compact contract only at the one spot that genuinely needs it - here, the final ticket-to-token conversion. Start on offer files for simplicity; add Compact only where the construction truly demands it. It keeps the trusted, audit-heavy surface small, and it keeps your users on the cheap, familiar path for everything that doesn't strictly require more.

That's the native zone - and notice the progression it walked: list a thing you have; collect bids on a thing you haven't listed; collect pledges for a thing that doesn't exist. All of it runs on exactly the machinery of the first half of this post - fill rarely, cancel rarely, live for days. If your app lives here, you can build it today; the only thing you still owe yourself is the business case at the end. Everyone else: let's leave the comfort zone, slow direction first.

Long-term · Outliving the chain's memory

A listing is a process, not a file

Now sell something genuinely illiquid - a house, a stake in a private company - where the right buyer may appear in a year and a half. The collision is immediate: Midnight caps every offer at two weeks of life, so a long-term listing cannot be one offer file. It has to become a process. The straightforward pattern is a refresh series: before the current file expires, the wallet mints a fresh one - same terms, new expiry. Creation is off-chain and free; the only recurring cost is a proof every two weeks (tens of seconds even in a browser), which a wallet can run unattended.

The second pattern is simpler still: keep no live offer. Publish the terms as an ordinary listing, and mint the offer file on the spot when a real buyer appears - a request-for-quote flow. You give up the headline magic (a stranger settling without ever speaking to you), but for a house you were going to speak anyway - and in exchange every offer is minted fresh, at the moment of agreement.

The venue inverts

Celestia's week of retention is the perfect bulletin board for the middle zone and exactly wrong here. But remember the throwaway line from the start of the post - an offer file is just text you can host at a URL. This is where it pays off. A long-term offer's natural venue is the listing itself: the property page hosts the current offer file, the refresh series swaps it in place, and discovery is the web - a listing site, a registry - not an order book.

Indexing collapses with it: no firehose, no namespace - to check one listing is live you verify one fact, that its backing coin is still unspent on Midnight. And the privacy fit is at its strongest at this end of the spectrum. The asset is public by nature - it's a house - but the seller's wallet and the rest of their holdings never surface. Settle through the private hand-off pattern and even the final price needn't go public: once a trade balances, the deltas peel off, so the chain records the transfer, not the terms.

What the file can't settle

Three honest costs of this zone. First, custody discipline: the backing coin must stay unspent for the listing's whole life, through months of ordinary wallet activity - one careless coin consolidation and the listing dies silently. That's a wallet feature, not a protocol one: the coin has to be earmarked, fenced off from everyday spending.

Second, price drift: a fixed price standing for months is the free-option problem of the DEX world in slow motion. The mitigations are mundane - denominate in something stable, and let each refresh double as a repricing moment. Note the irony, though: this zone leans on stablecoins, and on Chia, stablecoins were the one category that never took off.

Third, the elephant: an offer file settles the token, not the title. Binding a real-world asset's token to its deed is a legal wrapper, a registrar, a jurisdiction - problems no file format solves. What the offer file contributes is shrinking the trusted surface down to exactly that one binding: the settlement itself needs no escrow agent holding anyone's money. Fair warning that this whole zone is frontier - design sketches, not the reference design the rest of this post describes.

Short-term · Build a DEX

Now build a DEX with it

An order book almost falls out of offer files for free: each file is a resting order, and a marketplace is just a filter over them. For a taker it's ideal - browse, pick one, settle with a single on-chain transaction; listing and re-listing orders costs nothing. The catch hides in how Midnight represents a swap. An offer is built from a specific coin - a UTXO (unspent transaction output) - and the offer is valid only while that coin stays unspent (the coin-and-copies cascade from the anatomy).

So the lifecycle is lopsided. Creating and sharing an offer is free and off-chain. A fill is one on-chain transaction (settlement spends the coin). But a cancel is also an on-chain transaction - and that is fundamental, not a missing optimization. Once copies of your offer are loose in the world, the only way to make every one un-fillable is to spend the coin they all reference. There is no off-chain “unsend”: on a UTXO chain, cancelling is spending, and spending is a transaction.

The market-maker wall

A casual order book is one thing; a good one needs market makers - participants who quote a buy price and a sell price at once and earn the small spread between them. When two offers cross (someone selling a touch cheap, someone buying a touch high), a market maker fills both and pockets the difference. Repeat that continuously and you get tight prices and deep liquidity - the entire reason exchanges court market makers.

But quoting is never set-and-forget. The fair price moves constantly, and the moment it does, a maker's whole ladder is mispriced and must be cancelled and re-posted. Professional makers cancel far more orders than they ever fill - cancel-to-fill ratios above 90% are routine. On Midnight every one of those cancels is an on-chain transaction, and a ZK L1 clears only a handful per second with slow finality. A maker who needs to re-quote dozens of times a second simply can't - so serious market making is, quietly, impossible.

And that is the missing piece. Offer files give us a shared, off-chain order book and real settlement on the base layer - but for it to be a venue market makers can actually work, cancellation has to be fast and nearly free. The fix is a layer-2 (L2) built specifically for offer files. So let's design it.

Short-term · The L2

Designing the L2: integrity vs privacy

Building a L2 on a privacy chain forces two questions to the front. First, integrity: under what assumptions can someone prove they hold a balance on the L2 and withdraw it back to the L1? Second, privacy: who, if anyone, may see the L2's state? On Midnight privacy isn't a feature you bolt on - it's the premise - so both questions are in play simultaneously, and they pull against each other.

There's a useful reference point. Another Midnight project, Midnight City, already shipped a L2: they use the Ligero proof system for privacy, the Sovereign SDK to build the chain, and for integrity they run the sequencer inside a TEE (a trusted execution environment - a secure hardware enclave) that settles to a Midnight L1 contract. The L1 trusts the enclave's attestation that the L2 transitioned correctly.

That's a sound design - but ours is financial, and for money we don't want to rest withdrawals on hardware trust. A TEE is one disclosed side-channel away from forged exits. We want cryptographic integrity: a proof, not a promise from a chip. And on a private chain, that is genuinely hard - for a reason that comes straight out of how zSwap works.

The hard part: proving a private balance

On a zSwap chain, balances are private: they live as commitments and nullifiers that nobody can attribute to an owner just by looking. So when you go to withdraw, the L2 can't vouch for your balance - the sequencer genuinely doesn't know it. The proof has to come from you.

Midnight gives you the tool to start: a viewing key, which lets you (and only you) detect which swaps involved your address. But detecting your swaps isn't the same as proving your balance. To prove it, you must show - for every other swap as well - that it is not yours; otherwise you could understate deposits or invent them. Your proof therefore has to sweep the L2's entire history, not just your handful of swaps.

Even a modest L2 produces more swaps in a day than a user could ever fold into one proof - recursive SNARKs and all. You'd be able to prove only the last few minutes of history before the work explodes; the chain, meanwhile, runs forever. At any real throughput, the naive approach is simply dead.

So here's the corner: cryptographic integrity demands a proof, privacy means only you can build it, and building it naively means re-proving the entire chain for every user. It looks like a dead end - but there's a clever way out.

One proof for everyone

The trick is to stop making users prove their own balances. Have the sequencer prove once, for everyone at the same time. The catch: to compute everyone's balance the prover needs everyone's viewing key - exactly what must stay private. So the trusted enclave returns, in a far smaller role: each user sends their viewing key into the sequencer's TEE, where the keys can be used but the operator can never read them.

Inside the enclave, that one recursive SNARK can read every balance, so it folds the entire history exactly once into a single proof covering all users - the whole-history scan that was hopeless per-user now happens once for everyone. And because the proof is succinct, it reveals none of those balances: it only commits to them, and each user can later open their own slice of that commitment to withdraw, without re-proving anything.

And here is the elegant part - the trust split. The enclave is not vouching for the money; the proof does that, cryptographically, and is unforgeable even if the hardware fails. The TEE's only job is to keep the viewing keys private. So where Midnight City's design trusts hardware for integrity (a breach forges funds), here a broken enclave costs you privacy, never funds. Integrity is math; the hardware is only guarding the curtain.

So we have integrity and privacy: one proof in an enclave settles the whole L2 back to Midnight, cryptographically and without revealing a balance. That same proof does one more job worth pausing on - it's also how you get your funds back out.

Getting your funds back out

One question remains: once your funds live on the L2, how do you get them out, back to the L1? On the L2 you have more than create offer and cancel offer - there's a third move, offboard. An offboard is an unbalanced swap that locks the funds you want to withdraw and makes them unspendable on the L2. That imbalance - funds leaving with nothing coming back - is your exit amount.

Because the offboard is part of the L2 state, it rides inside the same recursive proof the sequencer posts to the L1. The L1 contract reads the proof, sees that this address offboarded this much, and completes the other half by paying the funds to the same address on the L1. Same address on both sides is the entire security: only the address that locked the funds can claim them, so no one can intercept your exit.

Step back and it's an offer file again - just split across two layers. One half lives on the L2 (lock and declare), the other on the L1 (pay out), bound together by a shared address. The pattern that opened this talk is also how you leave it. (Onboarding is simply the mirror image: lock on the L1, credit the same address on the L2.) With the exit covered, the core design is complete - and what's left is making it fast.

Short-term · Scaling

Scaling, part 1: the TEE off the hot path

Reassuringly, the enclave isn't in the way of anyone using the system. Recall that an offer file is transparent about the assets and amounts in a swap - it hides only the addresses. So the everyday path needs no enclave at all: a user hands their offer file to an ordinary sequencer, which orders it and surfaces it on explorers and order books right away. No special hardware on the path every user touches.

The TEE sits off to the side as a separate system: every so often it pulls the latest swaps from the indexer, decrypts them with the viewing keys, runs the single recursive proof, and posts it to the L1. It's the prover, not the gatekeeper - nothing waits on it. Keeping it off the hot path is a choice, not a requirement (you could run the whole sequencer inside a TEE), but the less code that must be enclave-compatible, the simpler the system is to build and audit - so we keep the trusted surface small.

It's worth pausing on what this design keeps public, because it's the quiet reason a zSwap DEX can actually work. Offer files reveal the assets and amounts and hide only the addresses - so a marketplace can show real volume, liquidity, and prices while never exposing who traded. That middle ground is deliberate. Earlier “fully private” DEXs hid the amounts too, and they've struggled to find a market for three compounding reasons: traders actively want to see how much liquidity is available and what the current price is, so total privacy works against the very people it's meant to serve; hiding amounts usually forces you into MPC or FHE machinery, a steep jump in complexity and cost; and producing trustworthy liquidity statistics - the thing users actually want - without leaking the private state is genuinely hard to do cryptographically. Public amounts with hidden addresses sidesteps all three: the stats fall out for free, and the only thing guarded is identity.

Scaling, part 2: consensus without trust

Start with consensus - how the L2 orders transactions and finalizes them. What makes this easy: because security is cryptographic (it's a ZK rollup - the proof guarantees correctness), consensus doesn't have to provide security at all. It only has to order transactions and agree they're final. A misbehaving validator can stall or reorder, but it can never forge state - the proof catches that. So we're free to choose consensus purely for performance and for keeping MEV low.

Three reasons a BFT system is the right fit here:

  1. Security is already cryptographic, so consensus is free to optimize for something other than safety.
  2. We care about performance, and BFT gives fast, deterministic finality by design - no waiting out probabilistic confirmations.
  3. We want to minimize MEV, and a known committee with threshold signing is a good foundation to build that on - MEV-mitigation schemes like fair ordering or encrypted mempools could be layered on top, though that's significant extra engineering, not something the consensus gives you for free.

Concretely you can sit anywhere on the spectrum above: a single sequencer to start, round-robin to decentralize cheaply, or a protocol like Simplex where a BLS threshold signature lets the L1 - and anyone - verify the agreed L2 state from a single small signature.

Scaling, part 3: abstracting away the data layer

The original Offer File proposal posts the files to Celestia, and everyone reads them from its namespace. That's a reasonable starting point, but it puts two frictions right in front of users. Celestia today has roughly 6-second blocks, but with instant finality - decent for a live order book. However, it drags a foreign token into the experience: to post an offer you need Celestia's TIA, or you lean on a batcher to pay it for you. Either way, the data layer is something users and dApp developers have to reason about.

Our L2 makes that whole concern disappear - the data layer drops out of view for everyone except the sequencers. Here's the shape of it:

Once the L2 exists, this all dissolves. Tools sync the L2 state directly, and by construction that state already contains every offer and every fill - so there's no Celestia namespace to scan and no DA token in a user's wallet. The L2 could still use Celestia under the hood for availability, or swap in another DA layer, or simply post its data to Midnight itself. That's now an implementation detail the sequencers own, not something users or developers ever touch - and the speed worry moves to the L2's own fast finality from part 2, which we control.

Scaling, part 4: same shape, cheaper cryptography

Owning the L2 buys one more lever. Because we control it end to end, the L2 can change its own cryptography without changing anything users see. An offer file's shape - its fields, its bech32m encoding - never changes (the proof bytes inside differ, but the format doesn't); what we're free to swap is the machinery underneath, starting with the hash function the zSwap circuits lean on.

SHA-256 is expensive to prove. Swap it for a ZK-friendly hash like Poseidon1 and k drops two steps on each circuit - spend from 15 to 13, output from 14 to 12 - which, at roughly a doubling per step, is about 4× cheaper per circuit. And because the offer-file format never changed, none of this is visible from the outside: the same files, the same wallets, on a materially cheaper L2.

The win compounds across a whole swap. Recall a swap costs N × spend + 1 × output (one output here); swapping the hash drops each circuit's k, so every spend and output gets cheaper at once. Drag the slider to pile on inputs and watch how much Poseidon1 saves on each platform:

A market maker has to reprice the instant the market moves - and how fast they can do that is bounded not only by block time, but by how quickly they can regenerate all the zSwap proofs behind their new quotes. Cheaper proofs are, directly, faster repricing - which, as the Chia receipts will show, is the whole game for a maker.

Short-term · Open questions

Open questions

That's the core design end to end - but it would be dishonest to stop without the problems we haven't solved. Three of them are worth stating plainly.

#1 - the nullifier set never shrinks

Most zero-knowledge systems, Midnight included, prevent double-spends with nullifiers. On a transparent UTXO chain you'd simply mark a spent output as spent. zSwap wants the same guarantee - each UTXO spent at most once - but without revealing which UTXO it was. So rather than marking the UTXO directly, spending it publishes a nullifier: a deterministic, private tag derived from that UTXO that reveals nothing about it. The ledger keeps the set of nullifiers - effectively the set of spent UTXOs - and rejects any nullifier it has already seen. The same UTXO always yields the same nullifier, so it can never be spent twice, and no observer can tell which UTXO a nullifier came from.

On the base layer that's tolerable. But the entire point of the L2 is high throughput - and every swap adds a nullifier that is never removed (commitment roots expire; nullifiers don't). At real volumes the set grows into the billions, and every proof has to be made against it. Keeping that state from blowing up, without surrendering the privacy nullifiers buy us, is a genuinely open problem.

#2 - pricing and ordering transactions

How should the L2 order transactions, and what should they cost? Fees, first-come, an auction? It sounds mundane, but three forces pull against each other: (1) MEV & ordering - with no fees you tend to get off-chain backroom deals (quietly paying a block producer to jump the queue); a trusted BFT committee mitigates this by design, but only because you're trusting it. (2) Market-maker economics - real exchanges charge makers and takers differently precisely so makers aren't punished, and makers cancel and replace constantly, so any meaningful per-tx fee bleeds them. (3) Spam - set fees to zero and you invite junk transactions that slow proofs and (per #1) balloon the nullifier set permanently.

The best the figure can do - a low flat fee with first-come ordering - is the closest thing to an answer, and it still isn't clean. First-come ordering only stops bidding for position on-chain; off-chain, you're back to trusting the committee not to take a reorder bribe. And a single flat fee can't do what exchanges do with separate maker and taker fees - charge the spammer and the taker without taxing every maker re-quote. How fees and ordering should work on this L2 is still open.

#3 - how much to build, and when

The last question isn't really technical - it's about timing. How much complexity do we want? A perfect system takes longer to build, and this whole L2 has a shelf life, because two things could make it unnecessary:

  1. The Midnight L1 itself. It keeps improving, and it runs general-purpose ZK-optimized smart contracts - not just zSwaps - so if the base layer gets fast enough, you don't need this L2 at all.
  2. Nightstream, a more general Midnight L2 built as a full zkVM rather than a ZK-optimized construction; as it matures, the case for a zSwap-specific network shrinks.

But neither has arrived: it's unclear how long until the L1 matches a purpose-built zSwap L2, and Nightstream today has no clear path to connect to the L1 and is far slower than this L2 would be.

That points to a clear, if uncomfortable, answer: don't aim for perfect. With an open-ended window, the cost of over-building isn't just late delivery - it's shipping into a world that no longer needs what you built. Better to get something good to market soon, while the window is open, and iterate from there. Which is as honest a note as any to end on: a design that works, and the real questions still worth arguing about.

Will anyone trade?

Switching hats: would anyone actually trade here?

Everything above is engineering - how to make offer files work on a privacy chain, fast at one end of the spectrum and durable at the other. That is the easy half to feel good about, and the dangerous half to stop at: a protocol that is elegant and unused is just a clever way to spend a budget. So switch hats - from architect to investor - and ask the only question that decides whether any of this was worth building: will people actually trade on it? We can't prove the future, but we aren't guessing in the dark either, because this exact shape already ran as a live experiment for four and a half years.

The one thing we don't have to guess: Chia ran it

The dexie marketplace on the Chia blockchain shipped a conceptually identical offer-file system - wallet-created offers, a peer-to-peer gossip network, and shared liquidity across every site that indexed it. It is the closest thing the industry has to a controlled experiment on the idea, and the headline result is genuinely reassuring: it worked. Across 4.5 years and multiple boom-and-bust waves, dexie settled on the order of ~985,000 real, on-chain trades carried entirely by offer files. We pulled and analyzed 833,145 of them - the full breakdown is its own post, Chia Offer Files by the Numbers, which the rest of this section leans on. Offer files are not a whiteboard idea: they clear trades - though, as the next figures show, not many dollars of them.

Read those numbers the other way and they are sobering - and because a single dollar figure would mislead on a chain whose token fell from ~$30 to under $3, the honest, non-cherry-picked number is coin-denominated: the median trade held at about 0.2 XCH across the whole period (NFT prices were famously sticky at 0.1–0.2 XCH the entire time). In dollars the typical swap collapsed from ~$34 in 2022 to under a dollar by 2026 - as XCH fell ~15× and sub-cent game-token swaps flooded in - so it's a couple of dollars at most, small by any reading. The totals agree: ~1.08M XCH has ever changed hands, which - pricing each swap at the true rate on its day - is only about $16.9M of lifetime volume (a floor); the busiest liquid pair clears ~$800/day across ~17 trades; and nearly 90% of offers are a plain one-for-one swap. After 4.5 years, the honest description is a retail bazaar - real, durable, and small. The optimist's footnote is fair: scale is a property of the ecosystem, not the primitive, and Chia is a small chain in a long bear market. The pessimist's reply is equally fair: Midnight is a young ecosystem too, so there's no reason to assume it clears more out of the gate.

What it proved the primitive does well

Several of the strongest pro-offer-file claims survive contact with four years of data, and they carry over to Midnight because they are properties of the offer itself, not of Chia:

  • Trustless, atomic settlement. The trade completes in full or does nothing - no escrow, no counterparty risk.
  • Atomic multi-asset bundles. ~10% of offers carried three or more assets, with a tail to a single 32-asset trade - baskets an instrument-at-a-time order book can't express.
  • Safe composition into an aggregator. Because every route is one atomic spend, by 2026 roughly half of all dexie offers were multi-source routes; the worst case of routing through an untrusted source is simply that nothing happens (that same composability, we'll see, has a dark side).
  • A free price oracle. The settled-price tape reconstructs XCH/USD with a ~1.8% median spread, stable on as few as five trades a day.

One further result is striking but - fair warning - not portable the way those are: on Chia, trustless trading strengthened NFT royalties rather than eroding them (the trade-weighted median rose from 5% to 10%). That rides on Chia's NFT puzzle standard, not on the offer file itself, so whether Midnight's contract model reproduces it is an open question, not a carried-over win.

Read that chart against the spectrum map and it sharpens. NFTs, memecoins, game economies - everything that worked - lives in the medium-term zone: listings resting for days, cancels rare, the zone where Midnight needs no new engineering. The wreckage below (maker churn, pick-offs) is all from the short end. And the long end barely registers: real-world-asset NFTs were 0.3% of everything traded - silence, not a verdict. Keep that mapping in hand for the end.

What it warns about

The warnings are just as concrete, and they are about the market, not the mechanism. Given total freedom, the offer-file market converged on the architecture it was built to avoid. By 2026 roughly two-thirds of fills were taken by a single automated market maker (TibetSwap - 46% rising toward 67% of labeled fills), and discovery re-centralized on one aggregator: dexie routes about half of all offers - the flip side of the composability we just praised, the same force funnelling flow into a single venue. So much for the opening promise of liquidity shared permissionlessly across apps: the primitive stayed decentralized, but the market it produced did not. Worse for anyone hoping to provide liquidity, a resting offer is a free option you write to arbitrage bots. An offer priced in the taker's favor fills in a median ~38 seconds; a fairly-priced one waits ~10 minutes - textbook adverse selection. So makers couldn't earn a spread by sitting still; they had to cancel and re-quote constantly, producing eight to nine wasted offers for every one that settled (2.2M reward-earning offers in ten months, 99.7% of which never filled). And because none of that paid on spread, liquidity had to be rented with a token subsidy - which then shrank ~5×, from ~$1,700/month to ~$355, as the reward token itself fell. Whether that means the market was weaning off rented liquidity or the subsidy was simply eroding with its token, the data can't say - but liquidity still leaning on a subsidy years in was, at best, not yet organic.

Together, those two numbers prove the thesis behind Scaling, part 4. A maker's only defence is to reprice before the pick-off, and on a zSwap chain a new price means a new proof. So the binding constraint isn't just the on-chain cancel Chia paid for; it's how fast you can generate the zSwap proofs behind each fresh quote. Chia's receipts are, in effect, field evidence that zSwap proving time is decisive for efficient market making.

Now add Midnight

Here is where our specific chain changes the calculus - in both directions. The points above were argued chain-agnostically; Midnight is not neutral about any of them.

Midnight's biggest lever lands on the bear case's strongest pillar - but it's a lever, not a cure. The maker-killer on Chia was that cancelling is an on-chain spend, so makers churned millions of quotes and still got picked off. The L2 in the back half of this post makes cancelling fast and nearly free, which removes the cost of churning. What it doesn't remove is the cause: a stale quote still gets picked off in the window before you can pull it, and on Midnight every fresh quote has to be proven - so proving, not cancelling, may be the step that matters more, unless proofs can be made cheap or amortised across quotes. So the L2 makes market-making cheaper to attempt, not automatically profitable; whether makers come out ahead still rides on the L2's unsolved fee and ordering design and on how fast proofs can be produced. What does carry over cleanly is the rest: zSwap gives atomic settlement and multi-asset bundles in the base layer, and Midnight adds one thing Chia simply cannot - real privacy, hidden traders over public prices.

But Midnight also imports a cost Chia never paid. On Chia the whole DEX ran on about half a percent of block compute; an offer is cheap to verify. On Midnight the proof is still an expensive part - a single offer is ~16,000 characters of zero-knowledge proof, and settling a trade means a taker merges and posts that proof on-chain to be verified, with the matching overhead that implies. The L1 clears only a handful of transactions a second, and the nullifier set grows without bound. Those are real costs, and they cap throughput. But notice the shape of the bet, not just the price tag: in ZK the hard part is rarely the idea - it's finding one cheap enough to run today - and an offer file is about as light as a useful primitive gets. That it consumed a fraction of Chia's compute is actually encouraging here, because it's exactly the kind of lean primitive zSwap can afford under ZK now, where a general-purpose contract still can't. So the cost is genuine, but it's also why this primitive - and not a richer one - is the buildable bet. What stays unresolved is narrower: the fees and ordering questions still bear directly on whether makers can work, the economics that broke on Chia.

Then there's the AMM - the relationship most people read exactly backwards. Chia's market became an AMM: pooled liquidity, not resting offers, is what ultimately scaled. That invites the sharpest bear question: why build offer files if the market just turns into a pool you could have shipped on day one? But look at how it happened on Chia: the AMM didn't replace offer files, it plugged into them - TibetSwap fills offers and settles through the very same rail. Read that way, “the market became an AMM” isn't offer files losing; it's offer files succeeding as the substrate a pool runs on. And that's the likelier shape on Midnight too. Building a general-purpose AMM here means a general-purpose ZK contract - hard and slow, at least today - so offer files give you a working market now and let pools arrive gradually, as ZK makes them feasible, with the decentralized offer chain always underneath as the fallback rather than a cliff to transition off. Better still, because a new pool can bootstrap its liquidity straight from the shared offer-file pool, this path may even soften the winner-take-all gravity of AMMs - later entrants don't start from zero. The string attached: if pools never get cheap enough, offer files must carry liquidity alone, and Chia's verdict on resting orders still applies. So - a genuinely promising complementarity, with one real caveat, not a dead end.

Underneath all of this sits the question neither chain's data answers: who is this for? “Public prices, hidden traders” is not a universal want; it's a specific one. The plausible buyer is flow that must prove an execution price but not reveal who traded - OTC desks, treasuries rebalancing without being front-run, anyone allergic to MEV. That's a real niche, but it is emphatically not Chia's ~$2-a-swap retail NFT trader - which is the deeper reason to be careful extrapolating Chia at all: the mechanism may port while the user does not. But that gap cuts the other way too. Ask why the bigger players never showed up on Chia. Part of it may be that serious market-making is simply hard to incentivize at size - but part may be Chia's transparency itself, the very thing Midnight removes. If privacy is what kept large, identity-sensitive flow away from a fully public venue, then it isn't just a different user, it's a larger addressable market - one Chia structurally couldn't reach. Whether that demand actually shows up - far more than any throughput number - is the real, still-unmeasured question underneath all of this.

The honest verdict, zone by zone

Start where the receipts are thickest. The medium-term zone is proven: marketplaces, auctions, and launchpads cleared real trades for four and a half years, on a design Midnight can run today - no L2, no open research questions, and a privacy property Chia never had. It is also proven small: nothing in the data says this zone alone outgrows a retail bazaar. Build it first; just size the bet by Chia's numbers, not by hope.

The short end is where the real bet lives - the L2 of the back half of this post. Put the optimist's case in one breath: the shape is proven to clear real trades for years; Midnight's L2 directly attacks the defect that crippled market-making on Chia by making cancels cheap; privacy - public prices, hidden traders - is a real differentiator no transparent DEX has; and while general-purpose AMMs stay hard to build here, offer files may be the natural first liquidity primitive rather than a detour. And the Chia→AMM convergence may simply not bind here, because an AMM would struggle to offer hidden traders over public prices - so for the buyer who wants exactly that, there's little pool to lose to. If that reading holds, the move is the one the time-to-market question already pointed to - ship something lean and capture the window before it (or the moat) closes.

Now the skeptic's case, equally honest: the proven shape proved small - an ~$2-median retail bazaar - and it converged on an AMM and a centralized aggregator regardless of how trustless the rail underneath was. Chia's data is steady-state and silent on the hardest part, the cold start - and Chia still had to rent liquidity with a subsidy, so a younger Midnight, where every fresh quote also costs a proof, plausibly has to bootstrap harder, not easier. The maker economics hang on a L2 whose fee, ordering, and nullifier questions are open. And the pick-off that cheap cancels don't fully remove already has a rival answer shipping elsewhere - batch-auction privacy DEXs like Penumbra sidestep it by clearing in sealed batches instead of letting quotes rest at all. (That a competitor exists cuts both ways: it's a sharper rival, but also a sign the demand for private trading is real.) By this reading the bar for traction is higher on Midnight than it was on Chia, not lower.

And the long end is unmeasured - 0.3% on Chia is silence, not a verdict. It is the zone where the privacy fit is strongest and the competition thinnest, and the cheapest to try: a refresh series and a listing page, no L2 required. What gates it isn't protocol work at all - it's the legal wrapper binding a token to a title. A greenfield experiment, not yet a market.

Three verdicts, all built from the same receipts - and that is the point. They size three different bets. The middle of the spectrum you can build now, knowing it is proven and knowing it is small. The short end is a real engineering bet - the L2, and a demand no one has measured yet: whether enough people want a market that is transparent in its prices and private in its participants to bring real liquidity to it. The long end is a cheap experiment gated by lawyers, not cryptography. The architecture, we've shown, can be built - end to end, at every speed. Whether each zone should be is a bet - but now, at least, it's a bet you make with four and a half years of someone else's data in hand.

Appendix A: performance

How expensive is a proof?

An offer file is mostly proof - we saw it runs to about sixteen thousand characters because the zero-knowledge proofs dominate. So how expensive is it to actually generate one? That cost shaped many of the design choices in this piece, so it is worth pinning down precisely - here, in an appendix, for readers who want the numbers.

To compare how heavy two circuits are, you only need one number: a circuit's k. The intuition is simple - a circuit's proof time grows exponentially in k:

Technical aside - what k is, a little more precisely (most readers can skip this)

k is the size parameter of a zero-knowledge circuit. Midnight's proofs use a PLONK-style proving system (via halo2), in which a circuit is laid out on a grid of 2^k rows. A circuit needs some number of rows to express its constraints, and k = ceil(log₂(rows)) is the smallest power-of-two grid that fits them - so a circuit using 17,540 rows needs 2^15 slots, i.e. k=15.

Two cautions so the number isn't over-read. First, k is per circuit, not per app: one zSwap offer actually uses three circuits - spend, output, and sign - at k=15, 14, and 13 respectively. Second, k jumps in whole steps: because the grid must be a power of two, shaving rows only lowers k once you cross below the next power-of-two boundary. The takeaway is simply that lower k means cheaper, faster proofs.

A zSwap offer is built from two main circuits, and - since each coin carries exactly one token - the cost is simply a count of the coins it moves:

  • The spend circuit (k=15) runs once per UTXO you consume.
  • The output circuit (k=14) runs once per UTXO you create.

Hashing dominates the cost of both, and today that hash is SHA-256.

Recall that a transaction is just inputs and outputs, and an offer a proven subset of them (a few zSwap subtleties). Two things follow for performance. First, an offer file's cost is exactly the inputs and outputs it encodes - nothing else. Second, and less obvious: combining already-proven offers is nearly free. Merge two proven offers - even a one-sided one - and the parts already proven need no new proof; the expensive work happened once, when each piece was created.

That asymmetry is what makes arbitrage cheap. A bot can scoop up offers other people have already proven and combine ones that nearly cancel, leaving just a surplus in some token. It pockets that surplus with a tiny one-sided offer of its own - a single output paying itself, no inputs - so its only proving cost is that one output, no matter how many offers it stitched together.

Latency: from intent to settlement

How long does a swap actually take to go from "I want to trade" to settled on-chain? Assuming blocks aren't congested, the time required depends on three main steps:

  1. Proof generation - building the zSwap proof itself. This is the part we just measured, and it's by far the most variable: under a second on a native prover, tens of seconds in a phone browser.
  2. Landing on Celestia - the offer file is posted to the data-availability layer so it can be found and matched. Celestia produces a block roughly every 3 seconds (since its V9 upgrade), so you wait a few seconds at most.
  3. Settling on Midnight - the matched transaction lands in a Midnight block, which is where it becomes final. Midnight targets 6-second blocks, so this adds up to another six seconds.

Two of the three are fixed, small, and out of your hands - at worst under ten seconds of block latency combined, and on average about half that.

Throughput: offers per second

And how many swaps can the system carry at once? Two limits stack up: how much fits in a block, and how much fits in a single transaction. Both turn on the offer's on-chain size, which is just its pieces - each input, each output, plus a small fixed wrapper:

Block throughput

Start with the block. Each layer has a fixed budget - a block size and a block time - so dividing one by the other gives a ceiling. Drag the inputs and outputs to resize the offer and watch how many fit, per block and per second:

1 1 1×4,934 + 1×5,136 + 73 = 10,143 bytes
Layer Block Bandwidth Offer files / block Offer files / s
Celestia † data availability 32 MiB / 3 s ~11.18 MB/s ~3,308 ~1,103
Midnight settlement 2 MB / 6 s ~333 KB/s ~197 ~33

† Celestia's Fibre upgrade targets 1 Tb/s of blockspace - orders of magnitude beyond today's blocks.

The two layers play different roles, and both ceilings matter in their own way:

  • Celestia bounds how many offers can be posted - including the many that expire without ever settling, which Midnight never sees.
  • Midnight bounds how many can be filled, and it carries more per fill: the matched offer plus the settlement, and any offers routed outside Celestia.

So neither layer is simply "the bottleneck" - a market thick with fleeting, unfilled quotes leans on Celestia, while one where most quotes settle leans on Midnight.

Two caveats that mean these per-block numbers may be smaller in practice:

  • That bandwidth is shared - every other user of the chain draws from the same block, so a DEX only ever gets a slice of it.
  • These counts ignore per-block overhead - headers, encoding, and the other fields a block carries - so the real number is always somewhat lower.

Cost: per-offer fees

Remember, an offer file doesn't have to touch Celestia at all - you can hand it to a counterparty directly, or route it through some other channel. Go that way and, as long as you already hold the NIGHT that generates your DUST, posting costs essentially nothing. Celestia charges by gas: a one-blob post needs a fixed amount of gas plus a per-share amount (the PFB gas formula), paid at a gas price each node sets as a minimum filter - a 0.004 utia/gas default (the older default was 0.002; the protocol's own floor is far lower, 0.000001).
